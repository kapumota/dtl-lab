\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background-related-work}

The literature relevant to this study spans four closely related areas:
cross-shard transaction protocols, formal analysis of distributed and
blockchain systems, validation between formal models and executable
implementations, and reproducibility of experimental verification
artifacts. These areas already contain mature techniques that overlap with
individual components of our workflow. We therefore position the
contribution of this work in the integration and controlled evaluation of
multiple evidence layers for one executable cross-shard protocol, rather
than in the novelty of any individual verification technique.

\subsection{Sharded ledgers and cross-shard commit}
\label{subsec:related-cross-shard}

Sharded ledgers have long treated atomic cross-shard processing as a
central systems problem. Chainspace introduced S-BAC, a Byzantine
distributed atomic commit protocol for transactions whose objects span
multiple shards, together with correctness arguments and an executable
prototype evaluation~\cite{albassam2018chainspace}. OmniLedger introduced
Atomix, a client-driven atomic cross-shard transaction protocol that
coordinates input and output shards using proofs of acceptance or
rejection~\cite{kokoriskogias2018omniledger}. These systems establish that
atomic cross-shard commit and protocol-level correctness guarantees
predate the present work.

More recent systems focus on reducing conflicts, latency, or coordination
overhead. Prophet uses Byzantine-tolerant deterministic ordering to reduce
cross-shard conflicts and aborts and provides arguments for determinism
and serializability~\cite{hong2023prophet}. CSLAP builds leader
accountability on top of two-phase atomic commit and analyzes consistency,
liveness, and communication behavior~\cite{du2024cslap}. LightCross
combines TEE-assisted execution with a lightweight cross-shard commit
protocol and presents atomicity, serializability, and liveness arguments
for its design~\cite{qi2024lightcross}.

The cited cross-shard systems are therefore not studies without
correctness evidence. Their evidence is primarily expressed through
protocol design, analytical or theorem-based arguments, and system-level
evaluation using metrics such as throughput and latency. Those metrics
characterize the behavior of the protocol implementation and should not be
conflated with the resource cost of formal verification. In contrast, our
RQ4 characterizes the cost of bounded model checking under a predefined
experimental protocol. Mutation-based property validation and
implementation-model trace conformance are also not reported as
evaluation dimensions in the cited cross-shard works.

\subsection{Formal analysis of distributed and blockchain protocols}
\label{subsec:related-formal-analysis}

Formal modeling and model checking have been applied directly to
distributed transaction protocols. Schultz and Demirbas model MongoDB
distributed transactions in TLA+ and use TLC to analyze protocol
properties. Their modular treatment additionally generates tests from a
formal storage model and executes them against WiredTiger, providing a
direct connection between the model and a production implementation
interface~\cite{schultz2025mongodb}. This work is particularly close to
our study because it combines cross-shard distributed transactions,
bounded formal exploration, and implementation-facing validation.

Mutation-based assessment of formal specifications also predates this
work. Cordy et al. propose mutation model checking as a way to assess and
strengthen formal specifications by checking whether deliberately mutated
models are rejected by the property set~\cite{cordy2023mutation}. Their
work establishes mutation-based specification assessment as an
independent methodological line. Our RQ2 uses a smaller, protocol-targeted
catalogue of scientific mutants and interprets the resulting mutation
score only as sensitivity to those predefined defect classes.

The use of multiple formal encodings and deliberate defects is likewise
not unique to this study. Konnov et al. translate the executable Ethereum
3SF specification into TLA+, analyze it with Apalache, and use alternative
SMT and Alloy encodings for cross-validation~\cite{konnov2025ethereum}.
Their technical report also evaluates time, memory, timeouts, and
deliberately injected bugs. Consequently, neither the combination of
TLA+ and Alloy nor the combination of multi-formalism verification,
defect injection, and verification-cost measurements should be treated as
an isolated novelty claim in our manuscript.

\subsection{Specification validation and implementation-model conformance}
\label{subsec:related-conformance}

A substantial body of work addresses the gap between verified models and
executable implementations. Cirstea et al. reduce validation of
distributed-program traces against TLA+ specifications to constrained
model checking with TLC and provide an API for instrumenting Java
programs~\cite{cirstea2025traces}. Their framework permits partially
observed traces and makes explicit the tradeoff between instrumentation
detail and the search required to determine whether an observed execution
is compatible with the specification.

TraceLink further automates the relation between distributed
implementations and verified designs. It links instrumented executions to
TLA+ models, evaluates different trace-validation strategies, and reports
previously undetected implementation defects~\cite{hackett2025tracelink}.
The work also publishes a substantial experimental artifact and reports
partial external reproduction. Smart Casual Verification of CCF applies a
related philosophy to a production C++ codebase by integrating TLA+ model
checking, simulation, testing, and trace validation into continuous
integration~\cite{howard2025ccf}. These studies demonstrate that
implementation-model trace validation is already a mature technique in
distributed systems.

Other approaches generate or guide implementation checks directly from
formal models. Choreographic PlusCal projects distributed protocol
descriptions to TLA+ and generates conformance monitors, including an
evaluation on a two-phase commit implementation~\cite{foo2023choreographic}.
Mocket uses a verified TLA+ state space to generate systematic tests,
drive Java implementations through selected behaviors, and compare
runtime states with their formal counterparts~\cite{wang2023mocket}.
iMocket extends this strategy to incremental changes by restricting
testing to affected portions of the state space~\cite{gao2025imocket}.
Beyond TLA+, the Amazon S3 ShardStore study combines executable reference
models, property-based testing, failure injection, and stateless model
checking as a pragmatic multi-technique validation
workflow~\cite{bornholt2021s3}.

These results constrain the interpretation of our RQ3. We do not present
trace validation or implementation-model checking as a new general
method. Instead, RQ3 evaluates bounded implementation-model trace
conformance for a predefined cross-shard scenario catalogue, including
valid and deliberately corrupted traces and expected diagnostic
agreement. Its role is to provide one implementation-facing layer in the
same evidence chain used for RQ1, RQ2, and RQ4.

\subsection{Positioning of this work}
\label{subsec:related-positioning}

The reviewed literature shows substantial prior coverage of the individual
components used in this study. Cross-shard protocols already provide
atomic commit mechanisms and protocol-level correctness arguments
\cite{albassam2018chainspace,kokoriskogias2018omniledger,du2024cslap,qi2024lightcross};
TLA+ has been used to analyze distributed transactions and implementation
interfaces~\cite{schultz2025mongodb}; mutation model checking evaluates
the strength of formal specifications~\cite{cordy2023mutation};
multi-formalism blockchain verification can combine TLA+ and
Alloy~\cite{konnov2025ethereum}; and implementation-model validation has
been studied through trace checking, generated monitors, and
model-checking-guided testing
\cite{cirstea2025traces,hackett2025tracelink,howard2025ccf,foo2023choreographic,wang2023mocket,gao2025imocket}.

Within the comparator set for which full text was verified in our
structured review, we did not identify a cross-shard study that reports
all of the following within one frozen experimental protocol: bounded
property verification using complementary TLA+ and Alloy models,
targeted mutation-based property validation, bounded
implementation-model trace conformance over both valid and deliberately
corrupted traces, explicit verification-cost characterization, and
independent reproduction of the analytical artifact. This statement is
deliberately scoped to the reviewed comparator set and is not a priority
claim about the complete literature.

Accordingly, the contribution of DLT-Lab is best characterized as an
integrated and reproducible evidence workflow for an executable
cross-shard protocol. The study triangulates bounded formal verification,
mutation sensitivity, implementation-model conformance, and verification
cost under a frozen protocol, while preserving the limitations of each
individual evidence layer. This positioning avoids treating TLA+, Alloy,
mutation testing, trace validation, or artifact publication as novel in
isolation.
