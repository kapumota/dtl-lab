\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Bounded verification evidence}
\label{subsec:discussion-bounded-verification}

The RQ1 results provide bounded evidence that the cross-shard protocol
preserves the declared properties across the completed configurations.
No completed TLC or Alloy execution produced a counterexample for the
evaluated valid models. This result is relevant because the property
suite covers complementary protocol obligations, including replay
protection, receipt-dependent destination credit, decision
consistency, value preservation at termination, quorum enforcement,
terminal-state irreversibility, and release after timeout.

The strength of this evidence is determined by the explored bounds
rather than by the absence of counterexamples alone. Alloy completed
all three predefined profiles, whereas TLC completed the small and
medium profiles but reached the experimental timeout for every
large-profile measured execution. The TLC-large observations therefore
do not constitute property violations; instead, they delimit the
portion of the bounded state space for which verification completed
under the frozen resource policy.

This distinction is important for interpreting H1. The experiment
supports property preservation for the completed bounded
configurations, but it cannot extend that conclusion to unexplored
states or to the censored TLC-large executions. In particular, the
absence of a counterexample in the completed runs must not be
interpreted as an unbounded correctness proof.

The complementary use of TLC and Alloy strengthens the bounded
evidence without making the two analyses interchangeable. Each tool
encodes and explores the protocol using different formal semantics.
Agreement in the absence of detected violations across the completed
profiles therefore provides corroborating evidence, rather than a
claim that the two formal representations explore identical state
spaces or establish equivalent proofs.

\subsection{Mutation sensitivity and property adequacy}
\label{subsec:discussion-mutation}

RQ2 complements the absence-of-counterexample evidence from RQ1 by
testing whether the declared properties respond when relevant protocol
controls are deliberately removed. All ten predefined scientific
mutants were detected through their designated target properties, and
the detection outcome remained consistent across the repeated
measurements. The resulting mutation score of 1.0 therefore provides
evidence that the evaluated property suite is not merely satisfied by
the valid models, but is also sensitive to the injected defect classes.

This distinction strengthens the interpretation of the formal
verification results. A property suite that reports no violation for a
valid model is more informative when controlled perturbations of that
model cause the expected properties to fail. In this experiment, the
mutants exercise protocol mechanisms related to replay protection,
receipt-dependent credit, terminal decisions, timeout release, and
quorum enforcement. Their detection provides targeted evidence that
these protocol obligations are represented by the formal properties
used in the study.

The mutation results also expose the role of tool-specific formal
encodings. The commit-after-abort defect is associated with
\texttt{TerminalStateIrreversibility} in Alloy and
\texttt{DecisionConsistency} in TLA+. This difference does not weaken
the mutant-level result; rather, it shows that equivalent defect
intentions can be captured through different property formulations in
the two representations.

The interpretation nevertheless remains bounded by the predefined
mutation catalogue. A mutation score of 1.0 means that all injected
scientific mutants considered by the experiment were detected. It does
not establish that the property suite is complete with respect to
arbitrary implementation defects, modelling errors, or alternative
cross-shard failure modes.

Accordingly, the contribution of RQ2 is evidence of property
sensitivity and targeted adequacy, not completeness. Together with RQ1,
it distinguishes two complementary claims: the declared properties
hold in the completed valid-model configurations, and the same
verification framework reacts when selected protocol guarantees are
intentionally weakened.

\subsection{Implementation-model trace conformance}
\label{subsec:discussion-conformance}

RQ3 connects the formal models with the executable Java implementation
through bounded replay of observable traces. All 300 traces produced
from the predefined valid scenarios were accepted by the TLA+ replay
procedure, while all 300 deliberately corrupted traces were rejected.
For the negative catalogue, the expected diagnostic point also matched
in all 300 executions.

These results provide evidence that the abstraction used by the formal
model captures the implementation-level events exercised by the
predefined scenario catalogue. The result is stronger than checking
only whether valid traces are accepted because the negative catalogue
tests whether specific corruptions are rejected at the expected
abstract-action boundary.

The diagnostic agreement is particularly relevant. A generic rejection
would show only that a trace cannot be replayed. Agreement with the
expected rejection point instead provides evidence that the mismatch is
identified at the protocol step deliberately corrupted by the
experiment.

The conformance result nevertheless has a deliberately limited scope.
The replay procedure observes a selected abstraction of implementation
behavior, and the experiment evaluates ten valid and ten negative trace
classes over the frozen set of thirty seeds. Therefore, the result
supports bounded implementation-model trace conformance for the
evaluated catalogue rather than a statement about every possible Java
execution.

In particular, this evidence must not be interpreted as a formal
refinement proof or as behavioral equivalence between the Java
implementation and the TLA+ specification. Establishing refinement
would require a stronger semantic relation covering the full relevant
transition systems, whereas the present experiment validates
correspondence for the predefined observable traces.

RQ3 therefore contributes an implementation-facing validation layer:
the formal abstraction is not evaluated only internally through model
checking and mutation analysis, but is also confronted with observable
execution sequences generated by the implementation.

\subsection{Verification cost and scalability}
\label{subsec:discussion-cost}

RQ4 shows that the verification campaign is constrained not only by
logical correctness but also by the cost of exploring increasingly
large bounded configurations. The measured results exhibit increasing
cost within both formal tools, although the available evidence does not
support the stronger preregistered claim of nonlinear growth.

For Alloy, all three ordered bound profiles completed. Median elapsed
time increased monotonically from the small to the medium and large
profiles, and median memory usage followed the same ordering. The
Spearman rank association of 1.0 for both metrics therefore confirms a
monotonic relationship across the three observed profile levels. With
only three ordinal points, however, this statistic cannot identify the
functional form of the growth curve.

The TLC results expose a stronger scalability boundary. The small and
medium profiles completed and showed increases in elapsed time, memory,
and explored state-space size. The large profile, however, produced no
completed measured execution because all seventy runs reached the
frozen 1800-second timeout. This complete censoring prevents computation
of a completed-run median for the large profile and also prevents a
three-level characterization comparable to the completed Alloy data.

The TLC-large outcome should not be interpreted as an experimental
failure. Under the preregistered resource policy, timeout is a valid
censored observation indicating that the verification task exceeded the
available execution budget. Excluding these observations would hide an
important scalability boundary of the evaluated configuration.

The enabled-fault profiles provide an additional view of verification
cost. Within the TLC medium-bound configurations, the observed median
time and memory footprint differed across normal, insufficient-quorum,
replay, and timeout-oriented cases. These differences indicate that
verification cost depends not only on nominal bound size but also on
the behavioral structure enabled by a configuration.

The evidence therefore supports a descriptive conclusion that bounded
verification cost grows as the evaluated configurations become more
demanding. It does not establish a nonlinear or asymptotic growth law.
For Alloy, three completed ordinal levels are insufficient to infer
such a law, and for TLC only two profile levels provide completed
measurements.

Finally, the cost results must be interpreted within each formal tool.
TLC and Alloy use different modelling semantics, exploration
mechanisms, and internal representations. Their absolute elapsed-time,
memory, or state-space measurements therefore do not provide a sound
basis for claiming that one tool is intrinsically faster or more
scalable than the other.

\subsection{Cross-layer evidence triangulation}
\label{subsec:discussion-triangulation}

The four research questions are intended to provide complementary
evidence rather than four independent demonstrations of protocol
correctness. Each question addresses a different potential weakness in
the validation argument.

RQ1 examines whether the declared safety and liveness-related
properties survive the bounded interleavings explored for the valid
formal models. By itself, however, absence of a counterexample does not
show that the property suite is sensitive to relevant defects. RQ2
addresses this limitation by deliberately weakening selected protocol
controls and testing whether the designated formal properties detect
the resulting violations.

Conversely, successful formal verification and mutation detection
remain statements about the formal representations. They do not by
themselves establish that the executable implementation exhibits
behavior corresponding to those representations. RQ3 adds this
implementation-facing layer by checking bounded conformance of
observable Java traces against the TLA+ abstraction, including both
valid and deliberately corrupted executions.

RQ4 addresses a different limitation: whether the verification evidence
can be obtained as the experimental bounds become more demanding.
The observed increase in verification cost, together with complete
censoring of the TLC-large profile, makes the computational boundary of
the formal evidence explicit rather than treating verification as a
cost-free oracle.

Taken together, these layers form an evidence chain:

\begin{quote}
bounded property verification $\rightarrow$
mutation-based property validation $\rightarrow$
bounded implementation-model trace conformance $\rightarrow$
verification-cost characterization.
\end{quote}

The value of this chain lies in the different failure modes addressed
by its components. Bounded model checking can miss defects outside the
explored bounds; mutation analysis can only assess the selected defect
catalogue; trace replay observes only the chosen implementation
abstraction and scenarios; and cost characterization describes the
feasibility boundary of the evaluated verification configurations.
No individual layer removes the limitations of the others.

The combined result is therefore not a general proof of cross-shard
protocol correctness. Instead, it provides a reproducible,
multi-layer argument in which property satisfaction, defect
sensitivity, implementation-facing conformance, and verification cost
are evaluated under one frozen experimental protocol.

This integration is central to the methodological contribution of the
study. The formal models are connected to executable evidence and to
the resource conditions under which that evidence can be reproduced,
while the interpretation of each result remains explicitly bounded by
its experimental scope.
