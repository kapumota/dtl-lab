\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats-validity}

\subsection{Construct validity}
\label{subsec:construct-validity}

A first construct-validity threat concerns the relationship between the
TLA+ and Alloy representations. Both models address the same
cross-shard protocol obligations, but they express states, transitions,
and properties using different formalisms and analysis semantics.
Consequently, similarly named or conceptually related properties should
not be interpreted as syntactically or semantically identical
assertions across the two tools.

This threat is mitigated by defining the experimental claims at the
protocol-obligation level rather than requiring property-by-property
identity between the formal representations. The mutation experiments
make this distinction explicit. For example, the commit-after-abort
defect is associated with
\texttt{TerminalStateIrreversibility} in Alloy and
\texttt{DecisionConsistency} in TLA+. Detection is therefore evaluated
against the designated property within each formal encoding.

A second threat concerns the use of verification-cost metrics across
different model checkers. Elapsed time and maximum resident memory are
observable for both tools, but internal state-space metrics are
tool-specific and do not necessarily represent equivalent units of
formal exploration. TLC distinct-state counts, for example, should not
be treated as directly comparable to Alloy's internal search
representation.

To reduce this threat, cost trends are interpreted within each formal
tool. The study does not use absolute TLC-versus-Alloy timing, memory,
or state counts to establish tool superiority. Cross-tool observations
are used only as complementary evidence about the same protocol-level
verification problem.

A third construct-validity threat affects the implementation-model
conformance experiment. RQ3 does not observe the complete Java program
state. Instead, the implementation emits a deterministic observable
trace that is mapped to the action vocabulary expected by the TLA+
replay procedure. The measured construct is therefore conformance of
the selected observable abstraction, rather than full semantic
equivalence between the implementation and the formal specification.

This limitation is addressed by freezing the trace representation,
scenario catalogue, mapping rules, and replay procedure before the
definitive campaign. Both valid and deliberately corrupted traces are
evaluated, and negative cases require agreement with the expected
rejection point rather than only generic replay failure.

Finally, mutation sensitivity is an operational proxy for the adequacy
of the formal property suite. The ten scientific mutants exercise
selected protocol controls related to replay, receipt validation,
terminal decisions, quorum requirements, and timeout handling. They do
not represent an exhaustive taxonomy of cross-shard or implementation
defects.

Accordingly, the mutation score is interpreted only with respect to the
predefined mutant catalogue. A score of 1.0 supports sensitivity to the
injected defect classes but does not establish complete defect
detection or completeness of the formal property suite.

\subsection{Internal validity}
\label{subsec:internal-validity}

A first internal-validity threat concerns variation in host load and
execution conditions. Verification time and memory usage may be affected
by unrelated system activity, which could obscure differences among
experimental configurations.

This threat is mitigated by executing the definitive campaign on a
dedicated native Linux environment rather than on a virtualized or
shared CI runner. Tasks are executed serially with a maximum parallelism
of one, reducing interference among concurrent verification processes.

A second threat concerns startup and runtime effects that may influence
timing measurements. The cost experiments therefore separate warmup
executions from measured repetitions. Warmup observations are retained
as part of the execution record but are excluded from the statistical
summaries used to characterize measured verification cost.

A third threat concerns nondeterminism in scenario generation and
execution ordering. The experimental protocol uses deterministic seeds
and predefined configuration families. For trace-conformance
experiments, the definitive seed set is frozen before execution, and
scenario definitions are not changed in response to observed results.

A fourth threat is measurement instrumentation. Elapsed time and
maximum resident memory depend on the operating-system measurement
mechanism, while formal-tool outputs depend on tool-specific parsers.
Instrumentation failures could therefore be confused with scientific
outcomes.

To reduce this risk, instrumentation errors are classified separately
from verification results. A scientific execution is not retried merely
because its outcome is unfavorable. Retries are permitted only under
the predefined rule for instrumentation failure, preventing
result-dependent reruns.

Timeout handling constitutes an additional internal-validity concern.
A run that reaches the 1800-second resource limit does not reveal its
eventual completion time or final verification outcome. Treating such a
run as if it had completed at the timeout threshold would bias timing
estimates and could distort scalability conclusions.

The protocol therefore retains timeout and out-of-memory outcomes as
censored observations. They are not removed from the campaign and are
not converted into successful completion measurements. In particular,
the TLC-large timeouts remain visible as incomplete executions rather
than being replaced by selectively repeated runs.

Finally, the definitive experiment is governed by a frozen protocol
that specifies tool versions, bounds, configuration families,
repetitions, seeds, execution order, resource limits, and treatment of
incomplete executions. Freezing these decisions before interpreting the
definitive results reduces the risk of adapting the experimental design
to obtain a preferred outcome.

\subsection{External validity}
\label{subsec:external-validity}

A first external-validity limitation is that the study evaluates one
specific cross-shard commit protocol and its associated executable and
formal representations. The findings therefore characterize this
protocol design and experimental implementation rather than the full
space of cross-shard transaction mechanisms.

In particular, the results should not be generalized directly to
production blockchain systems with different consensus protocols,
network assumptions, cryptographic mechanisms, execution environments,
or adversarial models. The Java implementation used in the study is an
experimental simulator and validation artifact rather than a
production blockchain client.

A second limitation concerns the bounded formal configurations. The
small, medium, and large profiles provide controlled increases in
shards, concurrent transfers, validators, quorum requirements, receipt
copies, and formal-analysis bounds. These profiles are sufficient for
the predefined comparative experiment but do not represent arbitrarily
large distributed-ledger deployments.

Consequently, successful bounded verification within the completed
profiles does not imply that the same properties would remain
tractable, or that no counterexamples would emerge, under substantially
larger bounds or different protocol topologies.

A third limitation concerns the mutation catalogue. The experiment
contains ten scientific mutants selected to exercise important protocol
controls such as replay prevention, receipt validation, quorum
requirements, terminal-state behavior, and timeout handling. Other
defect classes may interact with the protocol in ways not represented
by this catalogue.

The mutation results should therefore be generalized only to the
evaluated defect classes and closely related protocol obligations, not
to arbitrary implementation bugs or adversarial behaviors.

A fourth limitation concerns implementation-model trace conformance.
The trace experiment uses ten valid and ten deliberately corrupted
scenario classes, each evaluated under thirty deterministic seeds.
Although this catalogue exercises multiple normal, timeout, replay,
quorum, concurrency, and corruption behaviors, it is not an exhaustive
enumeration of possible Java executions.

The RQ3 result therefore supports generalization to the evaluated trace
classes under the declared abstraction and bounds, rather than to all
possible implementation executions or workloads.

Finally, the verification-cost observations depend on the selected
formal models, tool versions, bounds, failure configurations, execution
environment, and resource policy. The observed cost trends identify a
scalability boundary for the evaluated setup, but they should not be
interpreted as universal complexity laws for TLA+, Alloy, or
cross-shard verification in general.

These limitations are addressed by restricting the claims to the
evaluated protocol, bounds, scenarios, mutants, and tool
configurations, and by preserving the complete experimental protocol
and artifacts required to reproduce those conditions.

\subsection{Conclusion validity}
\label{subsec:conclusion-validity}

A first conclusion-validity threat concerns the interpretation of
absence of detected violations. RQ1 reports no counterexample among the
completed bounded verification executions, but this observation is not
a statistical or unbounded proof of protocol correctness. In
particular, the fully censored TLC-large profile prevents treating all
scheduled RQ1 executions as completed successful verifications.

The RQ1 conclusion is therefore restricted to the completed bounded
configurations, and the hypothesis is interpreted with explicit
censoring rather than by treating incomplete executions as successful
property checks.

A second threat concerns the mutation result. Detection of all ten
predefined scientific mutants yields a mutation score of 1.0 for the
evaluated catalogue. However, ten mutation classes are not a random
sample from the universe of possible protocol or implementation
defects. The score is consequently a descriptive measure of sensitivity
to the injected mutants and should not be interpreted as an estimator
of detection probability for arbitrary future defects.

A third threat concerns the perfect observed classification rates in
RQ3. Each of the twenty predefined trace classes is evaluated under
thirty deterministic seeds, and all measured executions are classified
as expected. These repeated observations strengthen evidence for the
frozen scenario catalogue, but the seeds and scenario classes do not
constitute random sampling from the space of all possible Java
executions.

Accordingly, the observed classification proportions and their
associated interval estimates characterize the evaluated experimental
population. They do not provide a population-level guarantee that
arbitrary implementation traces will exhibit the same conformance
behavior.

The principal conclusion-validity limitation affects RQ4. Alloy
provides only three completed ordered bound profiles. A Spearman rank
association of 1.0 across three levels establishes monotonic ordering
for the observations but supplies insufficient evidence to determine a
functional growth form or to establish nonlinear scaling.

For TLC, only the small and medium profiles provide completed
measurements. All measured large-profile executions are censored by the
timeout policy. Consequently, the available TLC data cannot support a
three-level correlation analysis or estimation of a large-profile
completion-time distribution.

Censoring also affects descriptive summaries. Medians computed from
completed executions characterize only the observed completions. When
more demanding executions are preferentially censored, completed-run
summaries may underestimate the computational burden of the full
configuration.

For these reasons, H4 is interpreted as evidence of increasing
verification cost without sufficient evidence for the preregistered
nonlinear-growth claim. No asymptotic complexity law is inferred from
the three Alloy levels, the two completed TLC levels, or the censored
TLC-large executions.

More generally, the study emphasizes effect direction, bounded
property outcomes, mutation sensitivity, trace classification, and
transparent censoring rather than significance claims unsupported by
the experimental design. The conclusions are therefore limited to the
strength of evidence directly provided by the frozen campaign.
